Spectrum

Click anywhere on the image to measure the spectrum there. Colours = visit date; hollow grey = flagged pixels. Quick-look forced photometry with the SPHEREx PSF, deblended from Gaia DR3 neighbours; for research-grade spectra use the IRSA Spectrophotometry Tool.

Light curve

Median flux per visit and detector, normalised per detector. Variable stars change; moving objects stay flat. If no source is detected at the position, the raw fluxes (mJy, around zero) are shown instead. Click any star in the image to see its own light curve.

    Data-quality notes

      How to spot a mover

      • Blink: stars stay put; a dot that hops between epochs is moving.
      • Difference: static stars vanish; a mover leaves a dipole (dark/bright pair).
      • Boxes: automatic detections – pink mover? (dipole), blue variable?, yellow one visit only (asteroid, transient or artifact: check the asteroid overlay). They are hints, not discoveries – always look yourself.
      • D6−D4: cold brown dwarfs are bright at 4.5 µm but dark in the 3.3 µm methane band.
      • No Gaia match? A moving source invisible to Gaia is too cold for optical light – exactly how volunteers find brown dwarfs.
      • Beware artifacts: ghosts and cosmic rays appear in one frame only, or move with the detector instead of the sky.

      This tool does not register discoveries. Each kind of object has its own official service: the finder reports it there, and official names come from those services, not from the finder. First make sure it is not already known (Gaia DR3 and Known asteroids overlays) and not an artifact (Data-quality notes; seen in more than one visit).

      Moving Solar System body (asteroid, comet, distant planet)

      The IAU Minor Planet Center (MPC) ↗ takes measured positions (ADES format) from observers with an MPC observatory code ↗; new observers ask for a code and first show good measurements of known objects. In practice, send your finding to an astronomy club, observatory or survey team that reports to the MPC.

      A possible comet: also e-mail the IAU Central Bureau for Astronomical Telegrams (CBAT) ↗ at cbatiau@eps.harvard.edu, as plain text with the object type in the subject line.

      Names: once an asteroid is numbered, its discoverer may propose a name, which the IAU Working Group on Small Body Nomenclature approves. Comets carry the names of their discoverers.

      Brown dwarf or fast-moving star

      There is no official registry. Post it on the Backyard Worlds: Planet 9 Talk forum ↗ (Science board, Zooniverse account needed to post), where volunteers and astronomers discuss candidates and arrange follow-up with other telescopes. Volunteers who found brown dwarfs this way have been co-authors of the science papers.

      Names: catalogue designations built from the coordinates (e.g. WISEA J110125.95+540052.8). The IAU does not give personal names to stars.

      Variable star (changes brightness, does not move)

      Register ↗ and log in at the AAVSO International Variable Star Index (VSX), then use Submit ↗ to enter a new variable star. Moderators check that it is variable and new; its light curve is the evidence. A sudden outburst (possible nova) goes to the CBAT first.

      Names: VSX lists the star under a catalogue designation; the finder does not choose it.

      Transient (appears in one visit, does not move)

      Request an account ↗ on the IAU Transient Name Server (TNS) ↗ (it asks for your affiliation or, without one, why you need the account), log in and open Reports → AT report. It asks for the discovery brightness and the last time the source was not seen. Novae and comets: the CBAT.

      Names: TNS gives every new transient a designation such as AT 2026abc; your own name for it can go in its “Internal name” field.

      About SkyBlink

      SkyBlink turns NASA SPHEREx infrared images of any position on the sky into a short movie, so that anything that moves or changes brightness between visits stands out – from nearby stars and cold brown dwarfs to asteroids and comets.

      1. Enter RA/Dec or a name, or pick a challenge test target.
      2. Watch the visits blink; switch to Difference to see what changed.
      3. Click any source to measure its spectrum (0.75–5 µm) and light curve.

      Data: NASA SPHEREx Level-2 images (DR1, QR3) from NASA/IPAC IRSA, read from its public AWS copy; archival 2MASS and WISE; ESA Gaia DR3; JPL small-body data. Built for the NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026 (“Planet X and SPHEREx”). Not an official NASA product. AI coding assistants were used and are credited in the README.
